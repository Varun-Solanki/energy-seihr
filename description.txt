Project Title: A Dual-Layer Coupled SEIHR Framework for Simulating Energy Generation and Public Health Interdependencies

Summary

This project builds a computational system that models the feedback relationship between a region's energy generation mix and its population health outcomes. The core premise is that these two systems — energy infrastructure and public health — are deeply interdependent but are almost always studied in isolation. When a region relies heavily on fossil fuel-based electricity generation, it produces air pollution that drives population health deterioration. Rising health burdens in turn create political and economic pressure that shifts energy policy toward cleaner sources. This is a closed feedback loop, and modeling it mathematically is the central contribution of this work.

The mathematical foundation is an extended SEIHR compartmental model — the same class of differential equations used in epidemiological modeling — repurposed here with its original meaning intact. The population moves through five states: Susceptible (healthy, unexposed), Exposed (experiencing elevated pollution but asymptomatic), Affected (active illness, outpatient care), Hospitalized (severe respiratory or cardiac events requiring inpatient care), and Recovered. This health layer is coupled bidirectionally with a second variable representing the region's fossil fuel dependency and pollution intensity. When fossil dependency is high, the population is pushed faster into exposed and affected states. When hospitalization rates climb high enough, they create pressure that drives policy changes reducing fossil dependency over time. Both directions of this coupling are captured explicitly in the differential equations.

Multiple regions are modeled simultaneously and connected through what the project terms a transboundary pollution transport matrix — capturing the real phenomenon where emissions from one region's power plants affect air quality and health outcomes in neighboring regions based on atmospheric transport patterns.

The system is parameterized dynamically using a large language model combined with a retrieval-augmented reasoning approach. Rather than training or finetuning any model, the system builds a knowledge graph from historical data — structured records of past energy policy events, generation mix changes, air quality measurements, and corresponding health outcomes. When a new real-world signal arrives (a plant closure announcement, a policy change, a pollution report), the system retrieves the most similar historical cases from this knowledge graph and the LLM estimates the simulation parameters by reasoning from those analogies. This approach is fully interpretable — the system can show exactly which historical precedent informed each parameter estimate.

The system is validated through backtesting on real historical events where generation mix data, air quality records, and hospitalization statistics are publicly available. The simulation's predicted trajectories are compared against what actually happened, providing quantitative accuracy metrics. Over time, the retrieval system improves: historical cases that led to accurate predictions are weighted more heavily in future retrievals, and cases that led to poor predictions are down-weighted — a lightweight feedback mechanism that improves system performance without any model training.

The MVP demonstrates the complete pipeline on one historical event, showing the full cycle from signal ingestion through parameter estimation, simulation, scenario branching, and validation against ground truth data.
