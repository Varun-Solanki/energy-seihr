Environmental Burden of Disease from Coal-Fired Power Plants in Germany

This study estimates the health burden in Germany caused by long-term exposure to nitrogen dioxide and PM2.5 emissions from hard coal- and lignite-fired power plants in 2015. The authors focus on source-specific disease burden rather than total air pollution, asking how much disease could be attributed specifically to emissions from these coal power plants. The central conclusion is that both hard coal and lignite contributed a measurable burden of disease, with lignite producing the larger impact for both pollutants.

The methodology combines air pollution modeling, population exposure assessment, and health burden calculation. The researchers used the REM-CALGRID chemical transport model to estimate how emissions from hard coal and lignite plants contributed to outdoor NO2 and PM2.5 concentrations. Modeling was performed across nested European and national domains so that cross-border transport and chemical transformation of pollutants were represented. Power plant emissions came from German reporting systems and were scaled to the 2015 reference year, while other German and European emissions were included so that the coal contribution could be placed within total modeled air pollution.

The modeled pollutant concentrations were combined with spatially distributed German population data. The population data were based on the 2011 Census, aggregated to grid cells, and scaled to 2015. The authors then categorized exposed populations and calculated burden of disease using established environmental burden of disease methods. They reported years of life lost, years lived with disability, and disability-adjusted life years for outcomes where evidence supported an association with long-term exposure.

For NO2, the strongest-evidence health outcomes were cardiovascular mortality and COPD mortality in adults aged 25 and older. Hard coal plant emissions were associated with 5,695 years of life lost from cardiovascular mortality and 211 years of life lost from COPD mortality. Lignite emissions were associated with 13,051 years of life lost from cardiovascular mortality and 486 years of life lost from COPD mortality. In total, NO2 emissions from lignite caused about 13,537 years of life lost, compared with about 5,906 from hard coal, making lignite's NO2 burden roughly 2.3 times higher.

For PM2.5, the study considered coronary heart disease, COPD, diabetes mellitus, stroke, and lung cancer. Hard coal plant emissions were associated with 6,412 years of life lost, while lignite emissions were associated with 7,866 years of life lost. Coronary heart disease dominated the mortality burden, accounting for about half of the years of life lost for both coal types. Lung cancer contributed about 30%, while COPD, stroke, and diabetes made smaller mortality contributions.

The disability component also mattered. The study estimated 1,825 years lived with disability from PM2.5 emitted by hard coal plants and 2,239 from lignite plants. Diabetes mellitus was the largest contributor to this morbidity component, followed by stroke, COPD, and coronary heart disease. When mortality and morbidity were combined, PM2.5 emissions from hard coal plants produced 8,236 disability-adjusted life years, while lignite produced 10,105 disability-adjusted life years.

The paper finds that the disease burden for both pollutants is dominated by cardiovascular outcomes. It also finds that lignite causes a greater burden than hard coal, particularly for NO2. The authors attribute this difference largely to higher annual emissions of NO2 and PM2.5 from lignite-fired plants compared with hard coal-fired plants. For PM2.5, lignite's burden was about 1.2 times that of hard coal; for NO2, it was much larger.

The authors caution that PM2.5 and NO2 burdens should not simply be added together, because the two pollutants are correlated and the estimates are based on single-pollutant effect models. Adding them would risk double counting health effects. They also describe the estimates as partial and likely conservative because several potentially important coal-related health pathways were not included, such as heavy metals, ingestion routes, dermal exposure, and warm-season ozone effects.

The study's conclusion is direct: abandoning coal-fired electricity generation would reduce the burden of disease in Germany. The health gains would be especially meaningful for lignite-fired plants, which produced the higher source-specific burden in 2015. The paper also links this public health benefit to climate policy, arguing that shutting down coal power can reduce direct emission-related disease while supporting broader climate protection goals.
