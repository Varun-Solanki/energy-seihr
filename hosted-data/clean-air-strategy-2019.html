Clean Air Strategy 2019 Executive Summary

The Clean Air Strategy presents the UK government's plan to reduce air pollution from all major sources, improve public health, protect the natural environment, and support cleaner economic growth. It begins from the position that the country has already reduced many of the worst pollution problems through regulation, industrial investment, and a cleaner energy mix, but that air pollution remains one of the UK's largest public health challenges. The strategy stresses that harmful emissions now come from many diffuse sources, not only from large factories or road traffic.

The document identifies air pollution as a result of how the UK generates power, heats homes, produces food, manufactures goods, and moves people and freight. It focuses on five damaging pollutants covered by legally binding international reduction commitments: fine particulate matter, ammonia, nitrogen oxides, sulphur dioxide, and non-methane volatile organic compounds. The strategy sets out how national government, devolved administrations, local authorities, industry, farmers, households, and transport operators are expected to contribute to reductions through 2020 and 2030.

On health, the strategy emphasizes that air quality is the largest environmental health risk in the UK. Pollution shortens lives, contributes to chronic illness, and affects people through both short high-pollution episodes and long-term exposure to lower pollution levels. A major data point is the commitment to reduce public exposure to PM2.5. The government states that it will examine what would be needed to meet the World Health Organization annual mean guideline of 10 µg/m3, and that the policies in the strategy should reduce the number of people living in locations above that guideline level by 50% by 2025.

The strategy also treats air pollution as an environmental problem. It notes damage to waterways, biodiversity, sensitive habitats, and crop yields, with particular concern about nitrogen deposition. The plan links clean air policy to the 25 Year Environment Plan and calls for action on reactive forms of nitrogen, improved protection of habitats, and better integration between air quality and environmental governance.

For clean growth and innovation, the strategy argues that reducing pollution can also create economic opportunity. It supports development and adoption of cleaner technologies, industrial research, and policies that consider the trade-offs between energy, climate, and public health. The document connects clean air to broader strategies on industrial policy and clean growth, presenting pollution control not only as compliance but as a driver of new products, services, and manufacturing capability.

Transport remains an important source of nitrogen oxides and particulate pollution. The strategy points to cleaner road transport, the move toward zero exhaust emissions vehicles, stronger action against tampering with emissions control systems, and attention to non-exhaust particles such as brake and tyre wear. It also covers rail, ports, shipping, aviation-related emissions, active travel, and non-road mobile machinery, showing that transport policy must go beyond tailpipe emissions from cars alone.

Household emissions receive special attention because many people are unaware of their scale. The executive summary states that burning wood and coal in open fires and stoves accounted for 38% of the UK's primary PM2.5 emissions in the referenced national inventory. It also highlights sulphur dioxide from coal burning and non-methane volatile organic compounds from products used indoors. The strategy therefore includes cleaner domestic burning, better consumer information, standards for solid fuel appliances, and attention to ventilation and product emissions.

Farming is presented as the dominant source of UK ammonia emissions, accounting for 88% in the cited emissions data. Ammonia is important because it damages habitats and contributes to particulate pollution after reacting in the atmosphere. The strategy calls for low-emission farming techniques, support for infrastructure and equipment, better emissions inventories, potential regulation, and expanded permitting for some livestock sectors.

Industry is addressed through a mix of predictable regulation, sector roadmaps, cleaner technology, and tighter controls where justified. The strategy also emphasizes leadership at all levels, because pollution comes from local, national, and international sources. Local action is presented as necessary but insufficient by itself; national emission reductions, new powers, environmental principles, and clearer governance are all part of the intended implementation framework.

The conclusion of the executive summary is that the proposed actions can meet the UK's emissions reduction targets if implemented quickly and firmly. Its broader message is that clean air policy must now address smaller and more dispersed pollution sources, use health evidence more directly, and coordinate action across transport, homes, agriculture, industry, environmental protection, and local governance.
