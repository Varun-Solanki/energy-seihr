Source Sector and Fuel Contributions to Ambient PM2.5 Mortality

This paper provides a global assessment of which sectors and fuels contribute most to ambient PM2.5 exposure and PM2.5-attributable mortality. The motivation is practical: fine particulate matter is the world's leading environmental health risk, but effective air quality policy requires knowing which sources drive the burden in different places. The study covers 21 world regions, 204 countries, and 200 sub-national areas, allowing comparison across global, national, and urban-relevant scales.

The methodology combines atmospheric modeling, satellite-based exposure estimates, emissions inventories, and disease-specific health risk functions. The authors ran 24 sensitivity simulations with the GEOS-Chem global chemical transport model to estimate source and fuel contributions. They then linked those modeled source fractions to high-resolution satellite-derived PM2.5 exposure estimates at about 1 km by 1 km resolution, national baseline disease burden data, and concentration-response relationships from the 2019 Global Burden of Disease framework.

The study estimates that in 2017 the global population-weighted mean PM2.5 concentration was 41.7 µg/m3. About 91% of the world's population lived in places where annual average PM2.5 exceeded the then-used World Health Organization annual guideline of 10 µg/m3. The authors estimate 3.83 million deaths worldwide from annual ambient PM2.5 exposure in 2017, with uncertainty ranging from 2.72 million to 4.97 million. Most of these deaths were linked to ischemic heart disease, stroke, COPD, lung cancer, lower respiratory infections, type II diabetes, and neonatal outcomes.

A major finding is that eliminating fossil-fuel combustion emissions could have avoided about 1.05 million PM2.5-attributable deaths in 2017, or 27.3% of the total PM2.5 mortality burden. Coal accounted for just over half of that fossil-fuel burden, contributing 14.1% of total PM2.5-attributable deaths, while oil and natural gas contributed 13.2%. The paper emphasizes that coal remained the dominant combustible fuel contributor in several countries, including China and countries in Southern Sub-Saharan Africa and Central Europe.

Among sectors, residential energy use, industrial activity, and energy generation were leading contributors. Residential sources were associated with 0.74 million deaths, or 19.2% of the global PM2.5 burden. Industrial sources accounted for 0.45 million deaths, or 11.7%, while energy generation accounted for 0.39 million deaths, or 10.2%. The residential burden was strongly tied to solid biofuel use for cooking and heating, especially in South and Southeast Asia and parts of Sub-Saharan Africa.

The paper also finds large geographic differences. China and India carried the largest absolute numbers of PM2.5-attributable deaths, shaped by both pollution exposure and population size. The authors estimate that eliminating coal and oil-and-gas combustion in China and India alone could reduce the global PM2.5 disease burden by nearly 20%. In contrast, countries with lower PM2.5 concentrations can still have meaningful attributable mortality because health risks exist below 10 µg/m3 and because large populations or older age structures increase total burden.

At the sub-national scale, the dominant sources varied within countries and regions. The study shows that national averages can hide important local differences: residential sources may dominate one area, while industry, energy, transport, agriculture, dust, or fires dominate another. More than 65% of the 200 selected urban areas had population-weighted PM2.5 concentrations above their corresponding national averages. In some Indian urban areas, average PM2.5 concentrations were many times higher than the WHO guideline used in the analysis.

The authors discuss several limitations. Source contributions depend on emissions data, chemical transport modeling, satellite exposure estimates, baseline mortality data, and concentration-response functions. The study assumes equal toxicity per unit mass of PM2.5 from different sources, which is standard for the health burden functions used but may understate or overstate some source-specific effects. The simulations also estimate the effect of completely eliminating individual sources, so results should not be interpreted as exact predictions for smaller or combined policy reductions.

The conclusion is that PM2.5 control policies should be source-specific and geographically targeted. Fossil-fuel combustion, especially coal, offers a large potential health benefit if reduced, but residential solid biofuel, industry, energy generation, dust, transport, agriculture, waste, fires, solvent use, and shipping can all matter depending on location. The paper argues that transitions away from traditional high-emission energy sources could produce substantial public health gains, particularly in regions where anthropogenic combustion dominates exposure.
