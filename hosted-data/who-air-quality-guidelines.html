WHO Global Air Quality Guidelines

The WHO global air quality guidelines provide health-based recommendations for reducing exposure to major outdoor air pollutants: PM2.5, PM10, ozone, nitrogen dioxide, sulfur dioxide, and carbon monoxide. The central message is that air pollution is the single largest environmental threat to human health and causes millions of deaths and lost healthy life years each year. The burden is now described as comparable to major global risks such as unhealthy diets and tobacco smoking.

The guidelines update the previous WHO air quality guidance published in 2006. WHO explains that the evidence base expanded substantially over the intervening 15 years, including studies from lower-pollution high-income settings and from low- and middle-income countries experiencing rapid urbanization and industrial growth. The newer evidence shows adverse health effects at lower concentrations than previously understood, especially for long-term exposure.

The methodology followed a formal guideline development process. WHO convened groups with defined roles, including a steering group, a guideline development group, systematic review teams, and external reviewers. The process defined review questions, conducted systematic reviews and meta-analyses, assessed certainty in the evidence, and used an adapted GRADE approach to formulate recommendations. Only evidence judged to have high or moderate certainty for pollutant-health outcome associations was used to set the main guideline levels, and the recommendations for the classical pollutants were classified as strong.

The guideline levels are not legal standards by themselves. They are intended as evidence-informed targets that governments can use when setting national or local air quality standards and policies. WHO also includes interim targets for places where current pollution levels are far above the guideline levels. These interim targets are not final goals; they are staged reductions meant to help highly polluted regions move toward cleaner air in realistic steps.

The recommended annual PM2.5 guideline is 5 µg/m3, with a 24-hour guideline of 15 µg/m3. For PM10, WHO recommends an annual level of 15 µg/m3 and a 24-hour level of 45 µg/m3. For ozone, the recommended peak-season level is 60 µg/m3 and the 8-hour level is 100 µg/m3. For nitrogen dioxide, the annual level is 10 µg/m3 and the 24-hour level is 25 µg/m3. For sulfur dioxide, the 24-hour level is 40 µg/m3. For carbon monoxide, the 24-hour level is 4 mg/m3.

The health rationale is broad. Air pollution increases illness and death from cardiovascular and respiratory disease and lung cancer, and evidence is growing for effects on additional organ systems and life stages. The guidelines also emphasize that population ageing and the global growth of noncommunicable diseases make air pollution control even more important, because more people are vulnerable to outcomes such as ischemic heart disease, stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, and cancer.

For particle types where WHO did not set numerical guideline levels, the report gives good practice statements. Black carbon or elemental carbon, ultrafine particles, and sand and dust storm particles are recognized as important concerns, but the evidence was not sufficient to derive full quantitative air quality guideline values. WHO recommends monitoring, exposure assessment, source control where possible, and risk communication for these particle types.

Implementation is presented as a policy process, not just a measurement exercise. Countries are encouraged to assess national needs and capacity, translate the guideline levels into enforceable standards where appropriate, build air quality management systems, and use health risk assessment to compare policy options. WHO stresses the role of the health sector in synthesizing evidence, communicating risks, monitoring impacts, and supporting decision-making, while also making clear that air pollution control requires cooperation across transport, energy, housing, industry, agriculture, urban planning, and environmental agencies.

The conclusion of the guidelines is that reducing air pollution will deliver large health benefits, but progress depends on sustained political commitment and coordinated action. The guideline values show that many existing air quality standards are not sufficiently protective by current health evidence. WHO therefore frames the document as a decision-support tool for governments seeking to reduce avoidable disease, protect vulnerable populations, and align air quality action with climate and sustainable development goals.
